# mc-intel-meter — plans, operations and deception

Open this when the chapter contains a plan the reader is shown, an operation being executed, or
somebody lying to somebody who might catch it.

`references/writing-intelligence.md` covers deduction — how a character reaches a conclusion. This
covers the other half: what they **do** with it, which is where most of the visible evidence of a
character's intelligence actually lives.

---

## The withholding decision

Every planned operation faces one choice, and picking wrong is what makes heist chapters flat.

| mode | the reader gets | produces | costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open** | the plan in full, then watches it meet reality | dread and dramatic irony — the reader sees the flaw coming | no surprise at the execution |
| **Closed** | the outcome, then how it was done | surprise | the reader was a spectator; do it twice and they stop engaging with plans |
| **Partial** | the objective and the first two steps; one element withheld and **visibly** so | both | requires the withheld element to have been purchasable on the page |

**Partial is almost always right**, and the discipline that makes it fair is the same as a
mystery's: the withheld element was bought, borrowed or noticed in an earlier scene, doing another
job. A reader who can reconstruct it afterwards feels clever. A reader who cannot has watched a
trick.

**Closed is a cheat used more than once.** *He had arranged it three days earlier* is a campaign
that happened offstage — the past-perfect failure `story-craft` owns, in its most tempting form.

## The plan that survives contact

A plan that works exactly as stated is a plan the reader did not need to see. A plan that
collapses entirely is the author admitting it was scaffolding. The usable middle:

> **The plan's assumption fails, and the objective is achieved differently and more expensively.**

Design it that way. Before drafting, name three things:

1. **The objective** — what must be true afterwards.
2. **The assumption** — the load-bearing belief the plan rests on, stated in the scene where the
   plan is made, by the character whose belief it is.
3. **What the assumption turns out to be wrong about**, and who knew better.

That third line is where the opposition earns their keep. A plan that fails because of chance is
weaker than one that fails because somebody competent was also planning — `conflict-engine` owns
the antagonist standard, and this is where it pays out.

## Tier and the plan's shape

| tier | the plan | what the failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | one or two steps, stated aloud | it was always going to be enough or it wasn't |
| 3 | three steps and a fallback | the fallback is also based on the wrong assumption |
| 4 | branches; contingencies for the opponent's likely moves | the opponent does something *irrational* and correct |
| 5 | the plan includes being found out at the right moment | the thing that could not be modelled — somebody's loyalty, somebody's grief |

The tier-4 and tier-5 rows are the ones worth designing around, because they are the failures that
do not insult the character.

## Deception

A lie is a plan with one step, and it takes the same scaffolding.

**What makes a lie readable on the page:** the reader knows it is a lie, or strongly suspects.
Watching somebody be deceived with the reader also in the dark produces a reveal, not a scene —
and the scene is worth more. Put the reader ahead (`mystery-clues` calls this mode `ahead`, and
owns the rotation where that module is on).

**Four things a lie needs:**

- a **reason this specific lie** rather than silence — silence is usually safer, so lying is a
  choice that characterises;
- something **true** in it, because that is what people actually do and what makes it hold;
- a **cost if it holds** — a lie that succeeds cleanly costs the liar nothing and the story
  nothing;
- somebody in the room with the `eq` to catch it, or the explicit absence of one.

That last point is the seam with `social-perception`, which owns who can read whom. **A lie is
only tense when somebody present could see through it.** Put that person in the room, give them a
reason not to say anything, and the scene runs itself.

**Being caught is better than not being caught**, usually, and much better than being caught
later. A lie exposed in the scene forces a decision; a lie exposed in chapter 40 is a payoff the
reader has stopped waiting for (`plot-threads` on ageing).

## The floor still applies

Everything in `SKILL.md` §The floor binds here. In particular: the MC does not explain their plan
to an enemy who then escapes with it, and does not walk into a trap the reader saw coming unless
their own reason for walking in is stated and defensible. If a plan requires the MC to be
uncharacteristically incurious, the plan is wrong, not the character.
