# scene-craft — scenes with three or more speakers

Open this when a scene has three or more people who speak. A two-hander needs none of it; at three
the scene changes category, and the techniques that hold a conversation together stop working.

The failure is specific and it has two halves. Either the scene becomes a **queue** — everybody
gets a line in rotation, in the order they were introduced, and nobody interrupts — or it becomes
a **fog**, where the reader loses who is where and starts skimming to the next scene break.

---

## Before drafting: the three roles

Assign every speaker one of these. **Nobody gets two, and a scene with more than one driver is
two scenes.**

| role | what they do | how many |
|---|---|---|
| **driver** | wants the thing the scene is about; pushes | exactly one |
| **blocker** | wants something incompatible; resists | one, sometimes two |
| **pressure** | wants something *else*, and their want intrudes | any number |

The pressure role is what makes a group scene worth writing rather than a two-hander with
witnesses. The third person is not there to agree with one of the first two; they are there
because they have their own business and it will not wait.

## Who does not speak

**The strongest tool in a group scene is a silence that the reader is tracking.** Decide before
drafting which person is not talking and why — they are calculating, they are not allowed to, they
already know, they are waiting for somebody to leave.

Then give them **one physical beat per page-ish** so the reader keeps them in the room. When they
finally speak it lands, and it costs nothing to set up. A character who is in a scene and is
neither speaking nor being kept alive on the page has effectively left it, and readers are
surprised when they turn out to have heard everything.

## Keeping four people straight

Readers track group scenes by **position and property**, not by name.

- **Block the room once, in one clause**, at the top: who is sitting, who is standing, where the
  door is. Never a paragraph. The reader builds the room from one sentence and will keep it.
- **Give each speaker a physical anchor** and reuse it — the one at the window, the one with the
  cup, the one who has not sat down. This is the same reuse principle as an ability's sensory
  signature, and it works for the same reason.
- **Tag more than you would in a two-hander.** The rule that dialogue should be identifiable
  without tags (`dialogue-voice`) is a rule about *voice*, not about withholding attribution. At
  four speakers, an untagged line is a puzzle, and puzzles break the scene's momentum. Tag, and
  let the voice do its work inside the line.
- **Move somebody.** A person who crosses the room re-anchors everybody else for free.

## Turn allocation

Do not rotate. Real group conversation is **lopsided and it tells the reader who has power**:

- the driver and blocker take most of the turns;
- somebody is talked over, and the text shows it (`dialogue-voice` owns interruption);
- two people have a side exchange the others are excluded from;
- somebody answers a question that was addressed to somebody else, which is an act of status and
  costs one line.

**Count the turns after drafting.** If they are within one of each other across four speakers, the
scene is a queue and nobody in it wants anything.

## The scene structure is unchanged

Goal, obstacle, turn, exit, and an exit that is not a plain yes. What changes is that the turn now
usually comes from the **pressure** character: the thing that was not the subject of the scene
becomes the subject. That is the shape group scenes are for, and it is why a council, a dinner or
an argument in a corridor is worth more than the same information delivered twice in private.

## Crowds are not group scenes

More than about five speakers is a crowd, and a crowd is written as **one thing with a texture**,
not as individuals — with at most two people resolving out of it and back in. A paragraph of
unnamed onlookers reacting is the crowd-reaction tic, and `mtl-detox` owns the ban.
