# Story structure — shape, proportion, and how a win gets earned

Open this when planning an arc or a novel, when a victory is landing unearned, or when a story has
no shape between "chapter 1" and "the end".

The toolkit has one story-shape artefact — `chapter-plan` §Arc rhythm, a 25-chapter arc template —
and nothing above or below it. This file is the layer it sits in.

## Three acts, and what the proportions are for

The common division is roughly **25 / 50 / 25**: setup, complication, resolution.

- **Act one** establishes the protagonist and the world they live in, then breaks it with an
  inciting incident that raises a dramatic question the rest of the story answers.
- **Act two** complicates: attempts, failures, escalation, and a midpoint that turns the story.
- **Act three** resolves the question act one asked.

**The proportions matter less than the function.** A long act one works if it earns the conflict
that follows; a short act three works if the climax resolves what the story promised. Do not treat
25/50/25 as a target — treat it as a warning that a story which reaches its climax in the first
third has not built anything to climax from.

For a serial, the act boundary that matters is not the novel's — it is the arc's, and the reader's
decision to continue, which happens far earlier. See `references/serial-pacing.md`.

## Try/fail cycles — how a win becomes earned

The oldest narrative engine, and the single most useful structural tool for a progression serial:

> attempt → failure → escalation → attempt under worse conditions → …

Outcomes are graded, and the two useful ones are:

- **"Yes, but"** — they get what they wanted, and it creates a new problem.
- **"No, and"** — they fail, and something else goes wrong as a result.

Plain "yes" ends tension. Plain "no" stalls it. `scene-craft` §The scene unit already encodes this
at scene scale ("Never plain yes"); the structural version is that it must also hold **across
chapters**, so a protagonist tries and fails at something *significant* before succeeding at it.

**Two failures before a significant win is a floor.** Failure is what makes the victory feel earned
and what buys the reader's sympathy. A protagonist who succeeds on the first attempt has been given
the win by the author rather than taking it.

This is also the correct answer to "the MC is never stupid" (`mc-intel-meter`): failures come from
missing information, opposed will or cost — a competent protagonist can fail twice without once
being an idiot.

## The midpoint

The midpoint turns the story; it does not resolve it. Standard forms: the protagonist's
understanding of the problem is proven wrong, a false victory reveals a larger threat, or a
reactive protagonist becomes an active one.

The measured machine failure is to introduce the major setback and the climax briefly and settle
both quickly, which flattens the arc — see `references/why-ai-rushes.md`. Pressure should be rising
*from* the midpoint, not discharging at it.

## The story spine

One sentence, answerable, that the whole book is about — distinct from `chapter-plan`'s per-arc
dramatic question and from `timeline-engine`'s `ending.contract`, which is a destination rather than
a question. If a chapter cannot be related to the spine, it is either subplot or filler, and the
skim test will tell you which.
