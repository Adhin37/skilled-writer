# Scene and summary — the pacing lever

Open this when deciding whether a beat should be played or reported, when a chapter reads as a
highlight reel, or when a draft feels rushed without being short.

## The two modes

| | scene | summary |
|---|---|---|
| where the reader is | inside it, moment by moment | outside it, being told |
| time | a specific time and place | compressed, elapsed |
| carried by | action, dialogue, sensory detail, a turn | narration |
| what it produces | investment, emotional weight | momentum, bridge |
| narrative distance | close | far |

Both are necessary — the craft question is allocation, never "more scene". A story that is all
scene has no momentum and no proportion; a story that is all summary has no weight anywhere.

**The heuristic:** pull toward scene as stakes rise; push toward summary when momentum needs a
bridge. If a stretch of time is central to the plot, contains a change in a character, or carries a
strong emotional reaction, it belongs in scene. If it would be tedious played out, or exists only
so the reader can follow the plot, it is summary.

## Scene and sequel (Swain)

Dwight Swain's *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965) is the standard structural account, and it
is the one to reach for when a chapter's rhythm is wrong rather than its content.

- A **scene** is a unit of conflict: **goal → conflict → disaster**. The protagonist wants something
  specific, is opposed, and ends worse off than they expected.
- A **sequel** is the unit of transition between scenes: **reaction → dilemma → decision**. It
  metabolises what just happened and generates the motivation for what comes next.
- Below both, at sentence scale, sit **motivation–reaction units (MRUs)**: a stimulus, then the
  response to it, alternating.

**Sequels are the pacing dial.** In a fast sequence, sequels are short or absent. To slow a story
down without stopping it, lengthen the sequel — the reaction, the weighing, the decision. This is
the mechanism the toolkit's `scene-craft` §The follow-through already names; this file is the fuller
version of why it works.

Skipping sequels entirely is what makes a chapter feel like a highlight reel: things happen, nobody
metabolises them, and the reader is never given the beat in which to care.

## The past-perfect tell

The most reliable mechanical signal that a scene was skipped is a plot event inside a past-perfect
clause. Ordinary past-perfect is fine — it is how English orders two past events. The defect is
specifically a **turn** carried in one:

| reported | what was skipped |
|---|---|
| "She had made this exact lie four times in six weeks." | four attempts, each with a want and a risk |
| "She had spent three weeks making it true." | a campaign of manipulation, entirely offstage |
| "By the time he arrived, the argument was over." | the argument |
| "Over the next month she learned to read the wards." | the learning, which is a skill-acquisition arc |

Each of these is a chapter, or at minimum a scene, compressed into a subordinate clause. Ask of
every past-perfect clause: *is something turning inside this sentence?* If yes, either play it or
delete the reference and let the reader meet the thing fresh. Never report a turn.

## When summary is right

Summary is not a lesser mode and this file is not an argument for expanding everything. Use it for:

- travel and transit where nothing happens
- the fourth instance of a pattern established on the first
- elapsed time in which nothing turned
- necessary plot connective tissue the reader must know but need not experience
- deliberately withholding — a beat you intend to return to later in scene

The failure mode in the other direction is real (over-elaborating trivia), it is simply not the one
machine-written fiction usually has.
