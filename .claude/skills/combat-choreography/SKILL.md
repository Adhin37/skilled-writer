# combat-choreography

**Gate.** Applies only if `novel.md` → `optional.combat-choreography` is `on`.

A fight is a scene: goal, obstacle, turn, cost. Choreography without stakes is furniture being
moved loudly.

---

## Before writing a blow

Decide these five. Writing a fight without them produces the exchange-by-exchange drift that
readers skim.

1. **What is at stake** — beyond survival. The reader assumes the MC lives; make the fight about
   something the MC can actually lose. Someone else, a secret, a position, a principle.
2. **How it ends** — who wins, and what winning costs.
3. **The turn** — the moment the fight becomes a different fight than it started as.
4. **The terrain** — three concrete features that will be used. Terrain is what makes a fight
   specific rather than generic.
5. **The asymmetry** — what each side is better at. A fight between two people good at the same
   thing is a coin flip and reads as one.

## Writing

**Spatial legibility above all.** The reader must always know where the bodies are, where the
exits are, and what is between them. Re-anchor at every turn.

**Short sentences, short paragraphs.** Fights are the one place where uniform brevity is right.
Long subordinated sentences slow the eye exactly when it should be moving.

**Cut the exchange-by-exchange.** Do not narrate every strike. Compress the routine
(*Three exchanges. He was faster than she had assumed.*) and spend the words on the decisive
moments. The rhythm is: compress, compress, expand on the beat that matters.

**Intention over technique.** What a character is *trying to do* is legible; the name of the
technique is not. Not "he used Third Form, Rising Crane" but "he went for the elbow, because the
elbow would end it and he wanted it ended."

**Consequences accumulate within the fight.** Injuries persist and change what is possible. A
character who takes a knee to the ribs in the first exchange is a different fighter for the rest
of the scene. Track it.

**Fatigue is real.** Fights are short. Untrained people are exhausted in under a minute; trained
people pace themselves and it shows.

**Fear and adrenaline.** Time distortion, tunnel vision, the shake afterwards, the inability to
remember the middle. These are more evocative than any strike description.

**One idea per beat.** Do not stack a technique, a taunt, a sensory detail and an interior
thought in the same paragraph.

**Power use pays its cost on the page** (`power-system`), in the same scene, visibly.

## After

The aftermath is where the fight becomes a story. Injuries are treated or not, and persist across
chapters (log in the CCS block and the character's continuity facts). Bodies are dealt with by
someone. Killing costs something, and the cost is different the first time. Witnesses have
opinions and act on them.

**A fight that changes nothing should not have been written.** Cut it or attach a consequence.

## Anti-patterns

| pattern | fix |
|---|---|
| Blow-by-blow with no turn | compress; find the moment the fight changes |
| Power-level comparison as the fight's content | asymmetry of skill, terrain and intent |
| Villain monologue mid-fight | people fighting do not have breath for speeches |
| The MC holding back to teach a lesson | condescension the reader has to sit through |
| Fights won by a new ability | `power-system`: seen used, and seen fail, first |
| Injuries forgotten next chapter | continuity facts |
| Crowd narrating how impressive it was | `mtl-detox` |

## Self-check

- [ ] Stakes beyond survival, named before writing
- [ ] The reader can draw the space at every moment
- [ ] There is a turn
- [ ] Terrain used, not just described
- [ ] Injuries persist within the scene and into the next chapter
- [ ] The winner paid something
- [ ] No technique names doing work that intention should do
