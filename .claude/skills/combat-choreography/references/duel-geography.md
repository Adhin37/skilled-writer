# combat-choreography — geography, weapons and the body

Open this while writing a fight that is not going the way it should: the reader cannot follow it,
every exchange reads the same, or it has become a list of moves.

The skill body decides the five things before the first blow. This is the material for the middle.

---

## Distance decides everything

A fight is not a place, it is a **series of ranges**, and most of the decisions in one are about
getting to a range where what you have works. Write the range and the reader has the geometry for
free.

| range | what works | what does not |
|---|---|---|
| **far** | anything thrown or fired; running away | everything else — this range is about closing or not |
| **long** | a spear, a staff, a horse — anything that keeps someone out | a knife is useless and its owner knows it |
| **sword** | a cut needs room; a thrust needs less | grappling; you cannot get the edge on |
| **close** | elbows, the hilt, the head, the knee | any weapon that needs an arc |
| **ground** | weight, leverage, and whatever is in reach | almost everything, and a second opponent ends it |

**The turn in most fights is a change of range**, forced by one side because the other side's
advantage lives somewhere else. That is the same decision as the asymmetry from the draft card,
played out in space, and it gives a fight a shape without a single technique name.

## Keeping the reader in the room

Four anchors, used sparingly and always in motion:

- **The exit.** Where the way out is, and who is between them and it. This single fact carries
  more tension than any description of the space.
- **The floor.** Wet, loose, uneven, crowded. It is the cheapest terrain there is and it makes
  every movement cost something.
- **What is at their back.** A wall is safety and a trap, and which one it is changes during the
  fight.
- **The third thing** — the fire, the drop, the child in the doorway. Fights get their shape from
  the thing nobody can afford to break.

Re-anchor at every turn, in a clause. A reader who has lost the room stops reading the fight and
starts waiting for it to end.

## What bodies actually do

Almost none of this appears in the corpus's fights, and all of it is free specificity.

- **It is short.** A serious unarmed exchange is seconds. An untrained person is exhausted inside
  a minute, and exhaustion looks like the arms dropping and the mouth open.
- **Adrenaline costs accuracy.** Fine motor control goes first — hands shake, catches do not
  latch, and complicated things become impossible at exactly the wrong moment.
- **People do not notice injuries.** A serious wound is often discovered afterwards, by looking.
  The reverse is also true: a minor one can end a fight because of where it is.
- **Time distorts.** The middle is missing afterwards. This is worth more than any description of
  the middle.
- **Hands get hurt.** Hitting a head with a fist damages the fist. Grabbing a blade costs
  fingers.
- **Falling is the most dangerous thing in most fights**, and stairs, edges and furniture do more
  damage than weapons.
- **Nobody keeps fighting past the point of being done.** They stop, or run, or freeze — and
  freezing is more common than either.

## Armour and weapons, briefly

The reader does not need the vocabulary; the writer needs the consequences.

- **Armour works.** It is the reason fights turn into grappling and knives — the question stops
  being how to hit somebody and becomes where.
- **A weapon is a relationship to distance**, not a damage value. Somebody with a long weapon in
  a small room is carrying a liability.
- **Anything sharp ends a fight fast**, in either direction. That is why knife fights read as
  frightening rather than exciting, and why one should be over in a paragraph.
- **Numbers beat skill.** Two competent opponents defeat one excellent one in almost every real
  case, and a story where that is not true has to say why on the page — usually terrain, a
  doorway, or the fact that the two are not coordinating.
- **Nobody parries with a blade edge**, and nobody blocks a swing with a forearm and continues.
  Small things, and readers who know feel them.
