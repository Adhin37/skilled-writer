# character-profile — non-human characters

Open this when a named character is not a person: an animal, a monster, a construct, an artificial
mind, a god, a thing wearing a body. `references/special-cases.md` covers antagonists, mirrors and
canon characters; this is the other axis.

The two failures are symmetrical, and most stories commit one while trying to avoid the other.

| failure | what it looks like | why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **People in costume** | a dragon with a grudge, a proud manner and a human sense of insult | every tool in the character toolkit is built for people |
| **Furniture** | a creature with no wants, which is scenery that can bite | avoiding the first one by removing the interior entirely |

The way between them: **the character wants things, and wants them for reasons the story does not
translate into human ones.**

---

## The three questions

Answer these before the standard profile, and let them constrain every field in it.

1. **What is this made of, and what does that make impossible?** A thing with no face cannot be
   read (`social-perception` — every observer drops a rung). A thing that does not sleep has no
   day. A thing that cannot leave the water has a geography. The constraint is more useful than
   the capability.
2. **What does it want, in its own terms?** Not the human translation. A territory, a temperature,
   a debt, the continuation of a pattern, a thing it was made to do and can no longer do. The best
   ones are wants that are *legible but not sympathetic* — the reader understands and does not
   agree.
3. **What does it not have a concept of?** This is the field that does most of the work. A being
   with no concept of lying, of privacy, of scarcity, of endings, or of *other minds* behaves
   consistently strangely, and the strangeness needs no explaining because it is load-bearing.

## The standard profile, adjusted

Fill the usual template, with these substitutions:

| field | for a non-human |
|---|---|
| speech fingerprint | may be **no speech at all** — then the fingerprint is a behaviour pattern the reader learns to read, and it must be as consistent as dialogue would be |
| emotional signature | still four entries, still legible from outside, and at least one should be **unreadable** to humans in the cast (`social-perception`) |
| `intel` and `eq` | set both. A high-`intel`, zero-`eq` mind is one of the most useful characters available, and an artificial one usually is exactly that |
| arc ladder | only if it can change. Many cannot, and **a character who cannot change is a constraint the cast develops against** — that is legitimate, and it goes in the profile as a decision |
| competence | narrow, like everyone's. Ancient does not mean informed; it usually means informed about a world that no longer exists |

## The bodies

A non-human body is the one place the description budget is worth spending, once. Fix **two
details and reuse them**, exactly as an ability gets a sensory signature (`power-system`): what it
does to the air, what it sounds like when it moves, what it smells of, where the eyes are or that
there are none. Reuse is what makes a creature recognisable; re-describing it every appearance is
what makes it a special effect.

**Scale is conveyed by interaction, not by measurement.** What it breaks by resting on it. What
part of it a person can see at once. How long it takes to turn around.

## Animals specifically

Animals are not small people and not machines. Three things keep them right:

- **They are consistent within their nature** and surprising within it — the reader should be able
  to predict roughly and be wrong specifically.
- **They are not moral.** A wolf is not cruel. Attributing a moral position to an animal is the
  fastest way to turn it into a symbol, and symbols cannot be in scenes.
- **The relationship is one-sided and the story knows it.** A bond with an animal works because
  the human is interpreting, and may be wrong. That gap is the whole pleasure.

Where the setting has intelligent beasts, they stop being animals and take the three questions
above in full.

## Gods and things that have been around a long time

Longevity is not omniscience, and the profile must say so. Declare `knowledge_scope: broad` with
a shape *and* a boundary, and bound the **access** rather than the knowledge — sealed, asleep,
answering once a decade, reachable only through a ritual somebody controls. `competence-map`
owns the clause and the failure modes; the note here is only that a long-lived character with an
unbounded channel dissolves every mystery in the book, and it is the profile's job to close it.

The other thing age does: **it makes the recent illegible.** A being that remembers three hundred
years does not thereby understand this year, and often finds it trivial in a way that is wrong.

## What still binds

`bias-guard` in full. A non-human species is not a people with a national character: "they are
all like that" is the essentialism ban in scales, and inventing the group does not exempt it.
Where a species is genuinely uniform by construction — a hive, a made thing, a single mind in many
bodies — that is a *fact of the world* that somebody in the story has to establish, not an
assumption the narration is allowed to carry.
