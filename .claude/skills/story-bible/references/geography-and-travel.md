# story-bible — geography and travel time

Open this when the story leaves the place it started, when a plan depends on somebody arriving, or
when a chapter has moved people between locations and you cannot say how long it took.

`revision-pass` Pass 1 checks that travel is plausible against `state/timeline.md`. This file is
what it checks *against*. Without it the check has no authority, and the failure it catches — a
three-day journey happening overnight because the plot needed it — is the one readers price most
precisely, because everyone has travelled.

---

## The distance table

Six to ten rows in `bible/world.md`, and no more until the story needs one. Distances in **days**,
not in miles: the reader never converts, and days is the unit that constrains a plan.

| from → to | days, on foot | days, fast | what makes it slower | what is in between |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the village → the market town | 2 | 1, with a horse | the river crossing after rain | the shrine, the toll post |

The last two columns are where the value is. **What makes it slower** is a plot lever: weather,
a crossing, a checkpoint, a stretch nobody walks after dark. **What is in between** turns a
transition into a place something can happen.

A journey with nothing in between is a journey that should be one sentence
(`story-craft` owns scene versus summary, and most travel is summary).

## The rates, set once

Pick numbers and hold them. These are reasonable defaults for a pre-industrial setting; change
them deliberately, not scene by scene.

| mode | per day | notes |
|---|---|---|
| walking, unencumbered | 25–30 km | half that in hills, a third in forest or snow |
| walking, with baggage or children | 15 km | and the pace is set by the slowest person |
| a group on foot | 15–20 km | it never moves at the speed of its fastest member |
| horse, sustained | 50–60 km | a horse ridden faster than this is a horse you are spending |
| horse, one hard day | 100 km | and it is no use tomorrow |
| relay of fresh horses | 200 km | this is an institution, not a traveller — somebody owns it |
| river, downstream | 80–100 km | upstream is often slower than walking |
| sea, with the wind | 150 km | without it, nothing at all, for days |

**Speed is an institution, not an attribute.** Anyone moving faster than the table is using
something somebody else owns — a relay, a ship, a road, a permit, a power. That ownership is free
plot, and it is where this table meets the mobility question `social-fabric` owns: who may change
their position, and by which route.

## Information moves separately, and usually faster

Write down one number: **how long news takes to cross the map.** `social-fabric` owns it as
question 4, and it is recorded there; it belongs in the same glance as this table because every
plan in the novel is constrained by both.

The useful consequence is that news and people arrive at different times, which is the engine of
half the reversals in serial fiction: the warning that arrives after the thing it warned about,
the rumour that gets there first and is wrong.

## What the map is for

Not completeness. A map exists to answer three questions, and a novel that can answer them needs
nothing else:

1. **Can they get there in time?** The deadline plot, which is most plots.
2. **What is between here and there?** Which is where the chapters are.
3. **Why is this place here?** Settlements sit on water, a crossing, a resource, a defensible
   height or a route. A city with no reason to exist reads as a set, and the reason is usually
   one clause that also tells the reader who is rich.

Everything else — the shape of the coastline, the names of the mountains, the third kingdom the
story never enters — is worldbuilding the reader will never test. `world-texture` owns what any of
it is allowed to do on the page: a sensory signature per location, a budget, and delivery through
friction rather than through description.

## Journeys as chapters

When travel does get a scene, it is not the landscape that carries it. A journey chapter works on
**constraint**: what they are carrying, what they are running out of, who is slowing them down,
and what they have to decide at the fork. Put a decision on the road and the road stops being
scenery.

And the standing rule from Pass 1: when a scene and the map disagree, **move the scene, not the
map**. Distances become load-bearing the moment a reader has priced one, and a map that bends once
stops constraining anything.
