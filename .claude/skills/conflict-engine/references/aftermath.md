# conflict-engine — the cost that persists

Open this when the last chapter took something physical or psychological from somebody, and this
chapter has to carry it. Or when a cost keeps being paid and keeps not mattering.

The chapter card asks what this chapter cost. This file is about the chapters **afterwards**,
where most of the value of a cost actually is — and where it is most often quietly dropped. A
price that stops applying was never a price; it was a scene ending.

---

## The rule

> **A cost that does not change a later scene was not a cost.**

The test is mechanical and worth running at every arc boundary: take the three largest costs of
the arc and name, for each, a scene in a *later* chapter where somebody had to do something
differently. If you cannot, the arc's stakes were decorative however hard they hit at the time.

## Injury, at three time-scales

Most stories write the first and skip the other two.

| scale | what it does | how it reaches the page |
|---|---|---|
| **the scene** | changes what is possible now | already covered — `combat-choreography` owns damage inside an exchange |
| **days** | pain, sleep, fever, and the hundred ordinary things that need two hands | a task that is now awkward; somebody helping and being resented for it |
| **permanently** | a capability is gone, or costs more | it is in the character's continuity facts, and it shapes what they volunteer for |

**The middle row is the one that carries the realism.** A character who is badly hurt and then
appears three chapters later at full strength has taught the reader that injuries are scene
decoration. One clause per chapter is enough: what they cannot do today.

And the plain constraint: things take as long as they take. A broken bone is weeks. A deep wound
gets infected as often as not, in most settings, and infection is a better plot than the wound.
Where the setting has healing, `power-system` owns what it costs — and the interesting
consequence is always what it *cannot* fix.

## Exhaustion, which is free and underused

Nobody writes a character who has not slept, and it is the cheapest way to raise the price of
every subsequent scene: judgement degrades before the body does, the character knows they are
impaired and cannot correct for it, and other people notice before they do. It is a legitimate
route to a bad decision that does not violate `mc-intel-meter`'s floor — the MC has not forgotten
anything, they are simply running on nothing.

## Afterwards, for the person who did it

Violence has consequences for the one who commits it, and they are specific rather than generic
guilt.

- **The first time is different**, and the difference is physical: the shake afterwards, the
  inability to remember the middle, an appetite that is wrong for hours.
- **It gets easier**, and somebody noticing that is a stronger beat than the killing was.
- **The reaction is delayed and arrives inconveniently** — three days later, over something
  trivial, in front of the wrong person.
- **What they do about it is characterisation.** Working, drinking, praying, being useful,
  becoming precise. That behaviour belongs in their emotional signature
  (`social-perception` owns it), and it should already be written down.
- **Other people treat them differently**, including people who approve.

None of this is a licence for a chapter of interiority. It is one or two beats, placed where the
reader is not expecting them.

## What it costs the group

Costs spread, and the spreading is where the plot is:

somebody has to do the work the injured person did · somebody has to decide whether to wait ·
somebody is blamed, fairly or not · a promise cannot be kept, and that promise was a thread
(`plot-threads`) · the money to fix it comes from somewhere, and that somewhere is
`social-fabric/references/prices-and-stakes.md`.

## Where this is not the right file

If `optional.grimdark-consequences` is on, that module raises the severity band and adds the
permanence rules — it owns what may never be undone. This file is the always-on version: even in a
hopeful novel, a cost that evaporates teaches the reader to discount the next one.
