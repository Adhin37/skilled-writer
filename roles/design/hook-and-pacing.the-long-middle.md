# hook-and-pacing — the long middle

Open this at any arc boundary past arc 2, or when a stretch of the novel is competently written
and nobody can say what it is building toward.

`references/arc-rhythm.md` covers the shape of one arc. This is the problem that only exists at
length: the opening is a solved problem with its own skill, the ending has a contract, and between
them is the part where serials actually die. `CLAUDE.md` §5 bans the flat middle; this is how it
arrives, which is never as a decision.

---

## The four ways a middle goes flat

| symptom | the mechanism | the fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Nothing is at risk** | the MC has outgrown every established threat, and the new ones have no history | raise **pressure**, not tier — `power-scaling` owns the gap, and a familiar enemy with new leverage beats a stronger stranger |
| **Everything is at risk** | crises accumulated because none was ever resolved | `timeline.crisis_cap`. Resolve one badly rather than carrying three |
| **The cast has stabilised** | everyone has found their role and their arguments are settled | somebody has to be **wrong** about something that matters, and act on it (`character-development`) |
| **The promises have gone quiet** | threads are open, none is due, and the reader has stopped expecting answers | pull a `due` forward and pay something on the page (`plot-threads`) |

The third is the most common and the least diagnosed. An ensemble that has stopped disagreeing is
a comfortable cast, and comfortable is indistinguishable from finished.

## The structural answer: the middle is not one thing

A hundred-chapter middle is not a long second act. It is **four or five arcs, each with its own
question**, and the only thing that makes it a middle is that the book's central question stays
open. Each arc therefore owes the reader:

- **a question it answers**, that was not the book's question;
- **a permanent change** to the board — somebody's position, a place, an institution, who is
  alive;
- **one thing closed on the page** (`plot-threads`);
- **a new question that could not have been asked before it.**

An arc missing the second is the one readers describe as filler, even when they enjoyed it.

## The escalation trap

The instinct at chapter 60 is to escalate. It is usually wrong, and it is where long serials
acquire the bigger-sky problem: each threat larger than the last until the numbers stop meaning
anything and the reader disengages from magnitude entirely.

The alternatives, in rough order of how well they work:

1. **Change what is threatened**, not how big the threat is. The same opponent, aimed at something
   the MC has acquired since (`conflict-engine` owns the stake ladder — it moves by what is lost).
2. **Make an ally the obstacle.** Not a betrayal: a disagreement where both are right.
3. **Cash a promise early.** Paying a thread the reader expected to wait forty more chapters for
   buys enormous goodwill and costs one plot line you can replace.
4. **Change the mode.** A siege arc after a travel arc; an investigation after a war. Reader
   fatigue is often with the *shape* of chapters rather than their content.
5. **Let the MC win something permanently**, and give them the administrative problem that
   follows. Responsibility is an underused source of pressure and it cannot be punched.

## Temperature across a long run

The temperature ledger is per-arc, and across a middle it also has a shape: an arc that runs hot
throughout is exhausting by chapter 70, and one that never does is abandoned earlier. Check the
distribution at every boundary with `sw arc`, and specifically for the failure that only shows at
length — **every arc having the same temperature profile as the last one.** Four distinct values
per arc can still produce five identical arcs.

## The reader's own clock

Two things are true of anyone reading chapter 80 who was there at chapter 1: they have forgotten
details, and they have not forgotten promises. So the middle carries a small standing obligation —
re-anchor the *stakes* rather than the plot, in a clause, roughly once an arc, and never as recap.
Somebody wanting the thing out loud does it better than any summary.
